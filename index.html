How this works, and what it can't do

The text is split into passages of about 100 words. Each passage is measured as a whole, the way detectors like ZeroGPT work, because clean AI writing rarely gives itself away in a single sentence. Each sentence then gets its passage's score, adjusted by anything specific to that sentence.

  • Style statistics: how evenly sentence lengths run, average word length, how many abstract nouns (-tion, -ment, -ity), trailing "-ing" clauses (", ensuring…"), and how polished the punctuation is.
  • Specifics: human writing tends to carry names, places, numbers, a personal "I", asides in parentheses and exclamation marks. Generic text without them leans AI.
  • Wording: words and stock phrases that language models overuse ("enhance", "crucial", "delve", "a testament to", "it's important to note").
  • Templates: "not just X, but Y", "Whether you're a… or a…", essay-style wrap-ups, formal transitions at the start of sentences.
  • Formatting: markdown bold and headings, bullets with bold lead-ins, emoji bullets, frequent em dashes.
  • Human signals lower the score: slang, typos, lowercase "i", repeated punctuation, uneven rhythm.
  • Hidden characters: zero-width characters, unusual spaces, direction controls, Unicode tag characters and variation-selector runs (which can carry a hidden message, decoded here), and look-alike letters from other alphabets.

No detector is 100% accurate, this one included. Formal human writing (legal, academic, corporate) can score high. AI text that someone edited or rewrote can score low. Claude and GPT don't put a detectable watermark in their text, so no tool can prove who wrote something. Hidden characters prove the text passed through some tool or editor, not that an AI wrote it. Use the score as a reason to look closer, never as proof against a person.

It doesn't name the model (Claude, GPT, Gemini). Their styles overlap too much to tell apart reliably.
